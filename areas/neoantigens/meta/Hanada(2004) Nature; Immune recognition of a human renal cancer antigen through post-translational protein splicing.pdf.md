## Abstract

CTLs cloned from a renal cell carcinoma recognise an HLA-A3-presented nine-residue FGF-5 peptide generated by protein splicing - post-translational excision of a polypeptide segment followed by ligation of the newly liberated ends. The process had previously been described only in plants and unicellular organisms.

## Summary

This is the discovery that a presented peptide need not be a contiguous stretch of its source protein. The consequence for neoantigen prediction is severe and structural: a pipeline that enumerates peptides by sliding a window along a translated ORF cannot represent a spliced epitope at all.

Every method in this collection that starts from somatic variants and generates candidate peptides by windowing is blind to this class by construction.

## Key points

- First demonstration of post-translational protein splicing generating an MHC-I epitope in vertebrates.
- The epitope is non-contiguous in its source protein, so window-based candidate generation cannot produce it.
- Identified from CTLs infiltrating a human tumour, so the epitope was immunogenic in a patient before it was explained.
- Implies the presentable peptide space is larger than the proteome-derived space assumed by prediction pipelines.

## Limitations

A single epitope in a single tumour: it establishes that spliced epitopes exist without establishing how common they are, and the prevalence of proteasomal splicing has been contested since. Identification was by transfection and CTL recognition, so the splicing mechanism is inferred from the product rather than observed. No method for predicting spliced epitopes follows from it.
