## Abstract

A review of splicing as a regulator of neutrophil development and of splicing dysfunction in disease. It surveys cis-splicing variation in individual genes and trans-regulation of global splicing outcomes during normal granulopoiesis - including intron retention as a regulator of granulopoietic maturation - and then examines the contribution of splicing dysfunction to diseases of neutrophil number, function and maturation, including severe congenital neutropenia, myelodysplasia and acute myeloid leukemia.

## Summary

Addresses the recurring puzzle it names directly: how perturbing a ubiquitous cellular process produces highly specific disease. Granulopoiesis is a good case because splicing there is demonstrably both flexible and tightly regulated, and because intron retention is used as a developmental control mechanism rather than appearing as noise.

The most interesting passage revises a standard assumption. Intron-retaining transcripts are usually taken to be nuclear-detained or degraded by nonsense-mediated decay, and in whole-cell measurements they are indeed lower-expressed and less ribosome-associated. But subcellular analysis and ribosome profiling find comparable intron retention frequencies in the cytoplasm, with those transcripts often engaged by ribosomes - so export does not necessarily mean degradation, and NMD's role as the mechanism of intron-retention-based regulation is less settled than assumed.

## Key points

- Uses granulopoiesis as a tractable case of how perturbing a ubiquitous process produces lineage-specific disease.
- Covers both cis-variation in individual genes and trans-regulation of global splicing during neutrophil development.
- Intron retention is a developmental regulator of granulopoietic maturation, not merely an artefact or error.
- Connects splicing dysfunction to severe congenital neutropenia, myelodysplasia and AML in one framework.
- Challenges the assumption that cytoplasmic intron-retaining transcripts are simply degraded by NMD - many are ribosome-engaged.

## Limitations

A narrative review from 2017-2018 with no systematic search or evidence appraisal, and it predates most of the AML splicing work in this collection, so its leukemia sections are the least current part. Its focus is granulopoiesis and neutrophil disease, with AML as one application among several, so the coverage of leukemia-specific splicing is not comprehensive. Much of the developmental evidence it synthesises comes from zebrafish and mouse models, including work by the authors, and transfers to human granulopoiesis by assumption. The authors themselves identify the field's central measurement gap: protein isoforms cannot yet be quantified at a sensitivity comparable to transcripts, so the functional consequence of most observed splicing variation remains unverified.
